# Business case: post-discharge follow-up program

**For:** VP of Quality and CFO · **Decision:** whether to fund a post-discharge follow-up program in the next budget cycle, and where · **Date:** November 2026 · **Request:** question 4 in [01_request_and_scope.md](01_request_and_scope.md)

> **Illustrative client group.** CMS data does not say which hospitals belong to which health system. "The group" in this document is an **illustrative client group** of 8 New Jersey acute care hospitals, drawn by a seeded, size-stratified rule (3 High, 3 Mid and 2 Low HRRP volume band; seed 42; `config.yaml`, `src/client.py`). It is **not an actual health system**. Hospital names identify CMS facilities only to make the example concrete. Results for them are model estimates from public data, not their own finances, and nothing here implies any relationship with them.

**Scope and conventions**
- **Data.** Three CMS Care Compare files for 3,055 hospitals: the HRRP file for the FY2026 program (performance period 2021-07-01 to 2024-06-30), Medicare Spending per Beneficiary (CY2024) and Hospital General Information. Penalties are estimated with CMS's formula. Nationally, the estimate puts 97% of hospitals in the same class (penalized or not) as CMS's actual FY2026 penalties.
- **Rules.** A program starting in 2027 is scored under the rules in force from FY2027: a 2-year CMS window, Medicare Advantage (MA) patients counted in the score, and the penalty charged only on traditional Medicare (fee-for-service, FFS) payments.
- **Money.** US dollars per year in a full-strength year (FY2032), unless labeled otherwise. Current payment and cost levels, not inflated forward. Six-year totals are undiscounted unless marked NPV (7%).
- **Terms.** *Excess readmission ratio (ERR):* CMS's predicted ÷ expected 30-day readmission rate for one condition at one hospital. *Penalty threshold:* the ERR above which a condition adds to the penalty; we use the national median ERR per condition, while CMS uses the median of the hospital's dual-eligible peer group, which is not in the Care Compare file. *HRRP volume band:* eligible Medicare FFS discharges over the 3-year window (Low volume < 400, Mid volume 400–999, High volume ≥ 1,000); it is not hospital size.
- **Numbers.** From `reports/key_metrics.json` and `data/processed/bc_*.parquet`, regenerated by `python run.py`. Figures computed for this document are marked with a note. Assumptions and sources: [assumptions.md](assumptions.md). Interactive version: dashboard page 6. Method walk-through: `notebooks/03_cost_model.ipynb`.

## 1. Executive summary

- **A group-wide program does not pay for itself through penalties.** The broad design (heart failure, pneumonia, COPD and heart attack; half of all Medicare patients; all 8 hospitals) would cost about $1.12M a year and avoid about $292K of penalty a year once fully counted. That is $0.19 back per $1 on the hospital P&L, a hospital net of −$906K a year. Its cost is higher than the group's entire expected penalty without a program ($644K a year), so no effect size makes it break even. Nationally, the same design would pay back at none of the 2,688 hospitals where it could run.
- **The best-placed pilot returns about $0.75 per $1.** A targeted program for higher-risk heart failure and pneumonia patients at Inspira Medical Center Mullica Hill and Palisades Medical Center would cost about $130K a year. At full strength it would prevent about 7 readmissions and avoid about $110K of penalty a year, for a hospital net of −$33K a year. CMS scores a window that closes 15 months before each payment year, so penalty relief starts only in FY2029 and reaches full strength in FY2032. Over FY2027–FY2032 the pilot's hospital net is −$501K.
- **Break-even needs more than the evidence supports today.** On the hospital P&L the pilot breaks even only if it cuts readmissions among enrolled patients by at least 14% (base case 10%, close to recent trials), costs at most $236 per enrolled patient (base $400), or payers return at least 27% of their savings. Of 18 one-at-a-time ±30% changes, only one reaches break-even (Medicare payment per discharge +30%). In 2,000 Monte Carlo runs, 12% end positive.
- **For payers and hospital together, the pilot is close but still negative.** Whole-system net is −$20K a year (broad design: −$479K). Penalties and FFS payments move money between hospital and payers, so they cancel out. What remains is about $15,200 of hospital cost saved per avoided readmission, against about $17,900 of program cost per readmission avoided. Payers save about $16,900 per avoided readmission, which is the basis for asking them to co-fund. Whole-system net is positive in 23% of Monte Carlo runs.
- **Recommendation: no group-wide program; a measured pilot only on quality grounds.** If the group wants to act, fund the 12-month pilot as a quality investment, with a concurrent comparison group and a co-funding request to MA plans and ACO partners. At month 12, stop unless five tests pass: at least 80% of enrolled patients received all three components; at least 90 patients enrolled; variable cost at most $400 per patient; no rise in ED visits, observation stays or mortality; and enrolled patients readmitted less often than the comparison group. Make the program permanent only with a measured reduction of at least 14%, or at least 10% with at least 27% payer co-funding.

## 2. The program

The program is a three-part transitional care bundle for higher-risk heart failure and pneumonia patients, with one face-to-face visit within 7 days.

**What patients get.** All three components are required.
1. **Medication review before discharge** by a pharmacist or nurse, reconciled with the patient.
2. **A nurse contact within 72 hours** of discharge, by phone or home visit, to check symptoms, medicines and the follow-up appointment.
3. **A clinic visit within 7 days**, booked before the patient leaves.

**Not phone-only.** In a 2025 meta-analysis, post-discharge contacts within 7 days (almost all by phone, most a single call) had no effect on 30-day readmissions: risk difference 0.00 (Boggan 2025). In heart failure, structured telephone support and telemonitoring did not reduce all-cause readmissions, while home visits and multidisciplinary clinics did (AHRQ/Feltner 2014; Van Spall 2017). The evidence on how many components a program needs is mixed: Leppin 2014 favored five or more, while Tyler 2023 found effects for programs with 1–7 components and none for 8 or more. The evidence against relying on phone calls is more consistent. In observational studies, follow-up within 7 days was associated with fewer readmissions for patients 65 and older with heart failure or heart attack (Balasubramanian 2025).

**Who is enrolled (pilot).**
- Medicare patients aged 65 and older, traditional Medicare and MA alike, discharged alive with heart failure or pneumonia, at hospital-conditions whose ERR is above the penalty threshold. At Inspira Mullica Hill that is heart failure only: its pneumonia ERR (0.995) sits just below the threshold (0.996). At Palisades it is both conditions.
- The highest-risk 30% by a readmission risk score at discharge. In the HOSPITAL score's international validation, the top ~38% of patients by risk had about 1.65 times the average rate of potentially avoidable 30-day readmissions (Donzé 2016). The model assumes 1.5 times (range 1.2–1.8).
- About 200 patients a year at full strength: 114 at Inspira Mullica Hill (heart failure) and 87 at Palisades (37 heart failure, 50 pneumonia). Without the program about 31% of them would be readmitted within 30 days, about 63 readmissions a year. The base case prevents about 7 of these.[^enrolled]

**Why heart failure and pneumonia.**
- **Size.** They are the two largest HRRP conditions: nationally, 74% of excess readmissions and 54% of estimated penalty dollars. In the illustrative client group, heart failure is the largest single condition in the penalty ($511K a year), and the two together account for $687K of $1.23M (56%).[^hfpn] More patients also means more of any improvement reaches the score (Section 3.5).
- **Weight in CMS's formula.** Pneumonia and heart failure have the largest DRG payment ratios of the six conditions: medians of 4.6% and 3.6% of a hospital's base operating DRG payments (CMS FY2026 supplemental file).
- **Evidence.** Heart failure has the strongest trial evidence (AHRQ/Feltner 2014; Van Spall 2017), so the model gives it 1.2 times the base effect (12%). Pneumonia rests on discharge-planning trials in older medical patients (Cochrane 2022: RR 0.89 for readmission within about 3 months) and on observational follow-up studies, so it gets the base effect (10%).
- **Left out.** COPD: no effect on 30-day all-cause readmission (Zhong 2022: RR 0.95). Bypass surgery and hip/knee replacement: sparse, inconclusive evidence (Tobiano 2025). Heart attack: volumes per hospital are small, so only 13–45% of an improvement would reach the score at the group's hospitals, and the evidence is mostly observational.

**The comparison design.** The program as first proposed (the "broad" design) enrolls half of all Medicare patients with heart failure, pneumonia, COPD or heart attack at all 8 hospitals, at average risk, whatever each hospital's score.

## 3. How the money works

The hospital and the whole system see different numbers, because penalties and payments are transfers between them. Four mechanics then shrink either the money at stake or the share a program can capture: lost margin, the FY2027 MA rule, regression to the mean and partial credit for smaller hospitals.

### 3.1 Two perspectives

The two views used throughout (annual US dollars):

| View | Formula | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Hospital net** | avoided penalty + payer savings returned to the hospital − margin lost on readmissions that no longer happen − program cost | What does the hospital P&L see? (CFO) |
| **Whole-system net** | hospital variable cost saved on avoided readmissions − program cost | Is this a good use of resources for payers and hospital together? |

- In the whole-system view the penalty and FFS payments are **transfers**. A dollar of penalty the hospital avoids is a dollar Medicare no longer collects, and a payment the payer saves is revenue the hospital loses. They cancel out, and only the hospital's variable cost saved remains. This view does not value patients' health or time, so it understates the program's value to patients.
- **Benefit-cost ratio** = (avoided penalty + payer savings returned − lost margin) ÷ program cost, i.e., dollars back to the hospital per $1 of program cost. It is not ROI. A ratio of $0.75 means the hospital loses 25 cents per $1 spent.

### 3.2 Money per avoided readmission

Table 1. Money flows per avoided 30-day readmission (US dollars, base case, all payers).

| Item | Formula | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital cost of one readmission (C) | HCUP, inflated to 2025 | $19,000 |
| Payer saves the payment (P) | 0.892 × C | $16,948 |
| Hospital loses P but saves its variable cost (V) | 0.80 × C | $15,200 |
| Hospital's lost margin | P − V | $1,748 (zero if the freed bed is refilled) |
| Payers and hospital together save | V | $15,200 |

The payment-to-cost ratio of 0.892 corresponds to MedPAC's FY2024 FFS Medicare margin of −12.1% (cost = 1.121 × payment). The 80% variable share sits in MedPAC's estimate that 75–85% of hospital costs vary with volume. The model applies the same amounts to MA patients, i.e., it assumes MA plans pay close to traditional Medicare rates.

### 3.3 FY2027+ rules: MA patients count in the score, not in the penalty base

- From FY2027, CMS scores a 2-year window instead of 3 years, counts MA patients in the ERR, and still charges the penalty only on traditional Medicare base operating DRG payments (FY2026 IPPS final rule; CMS FY2027 HRRP fact sheet).
- The score now spreads each readmission over a larger patient base than the one the penalty applies to. With 41% of Medicare patients in MA (New Jersey 2025; RUPRI), each prevented readmission moves the penalty about 59% as much as it did under the FY2026 rules.
- A program that serves only traditional Medicare patients would leave 41% of the scored patients untouched and move the score less. To reach a given score change, **enrollment must include MA patients**. Cost then scales with all Medicare patients (about 1.7 times the traditional Medicare volume); the penalty base does not.
- Effect on the case: under the FY2026 rules, the broad program would cost $745K instead of $1.12M and lose $509K a year instead of $906K. The pilot would roughly break even (−$0.3K a year, $1.00 per $1).[^fy26]

### 3.4 Regression to the mean: the baseline is a forecast

- Hospitals with the highest ERR today tend to look better in later windows with no program at all. In CMS's actual payment cuts, a hospital's FY2027 cut kept about 0.58 of its FY2026 cut (slope across 2,894 hospitals in both years; correlation 0.57). Among the tenth of hospitals with the largest FY2026 cuts, the mean cut fell from 1.38% to 0.96%, and 40% lost more than half of theirs. FY2027 also changed the rules, so part of this is the rule change. Published research attributes 74–87% of the ERR improvement at penalized hospitals to regression to the mean (Joshi 2019).
- The baseline therefore keeps half of today's excess: forecast ERR = 1 + 0.5 × (ERR − 1), and the same for the observed readmission rate (**persistence** 0.5; range 0.3–0.8). This roughly halves the penalty at stake: $644K a year for the group instead of the FY2026 estimate of $1.23M, and $249K instead of $485K at the two pilot hospitals.
- It changes the avoidable penalty much less, because the program lowers the ERR by about the same amount either way. If today's penalty continued unchanged, the pilot would avoid $122K a year instead of $110K, and return $0.83 instead of $0.75 per $1.
- It also means a before-and-after comparison would credit the program with improvement that would have happened anyway (Section 9.3).

### 3.5 Pass-through: smaller hospitals get partial credit

- CMS estimates ERRs with a hierarchical model that pulls low-volume hospitals toward 1.0 (COPSS-CMS 2012). So only part of an actual change in readmissions reaches the score. We measured this **pass-through** in the FY2026 file as n ÷ (n + k), where n is eligible discharges in the CMS window and k is fitted per condition (369 for heart failure, 414 for pneumonia; R² 0.997–0.998). About 20% of a change reaches the score at 100 discharges, and about 80% at 1,500. The fit agrees with published measure reliability: 0.73 for heart failure at 1,019 cases, against the 0.70 reported by Thompson 2016.
- In the pilot, pass-through is 67% for heart failure at Inspira Mullica Hill and 40–45% at Palisades. Under the broad design, The University Hospital (Low volume) would get 9–15% credit.
- Assuming full credit would overstate penalty savings: the pilot would appear to make +$26K a year ($1.20 per $1) instead of losing $33K.[^fullpt]

## 6. Timing: penalty relief lags program cost

Costs start in January 2027. Penalty relief starts in FY2029 (payments from October 2028) and reaches full strength in FY2032.

**How CMS timing works.** The FY N penalty is based on discharges from July of year N−4 to June of year N−2. It applies to payments from October N−1 to September N, 15 months after the window closes (42 CFR 412.152). So:
- The FY2027 window (Jul 2023–Jun 2025; penalties in effect now) and the FY2028 window (Jul 2024–Jun 2026) are already closed. No program can change those penalties.
- A January 2027 start first touches FY2029: its window (Jul 2025–Jun 2027) contains 6 program months out of 24, at half strength.
- From FY2031 (window Jul 2027–Jun 2029) every month in the window falls after go-live. The weighted share is still 87.5%, because six of those months are in the half-strength first year. FY2032 is the first year at full strength.
- Program cost, lost margin and readmission savings start immediately.

## 9. Recommendation and decision rule

### 9.1 Recommendation

1. **Do not fund the group-wide program on financial grounds.** It would cost about $1.12M a year to avoid about $292K of penalty ($0.19 per $1), and it loses $479K a year even for payers and hospital together.
2. **If the group wants to act on readmissions as a quality issue, run a 12-month pilot** of the targeted design at Inspira Medical Center Mullica Hill and Palisades Medical Center. It costs about $90K over the first 12 months (FY2027 budget: $68K) and $130K a year at full strength.[^budget] It prevents about 7 readmissions a year at the base effect. Budget it as a quality investment with an expected hospital net of about −$33K a year, not as a cost saving.
3. **Ask MA plans and ACO partners to co-fund before go-live:** at least 27% of the payer savings the pilot generates.
4. **Separately, review hip/knee readmissions at University Medical Center of Princeton at Plainsboro** before committing money there. CMS's actual FY2027 cut for that hospital fell to 0.04%, so check the FY2027 condition-level data first.

### 9.2 Pilot design

- **Sites and conditions:** Inspira Mullica Hill (heart failure); Palisades (heart failure and pneumonia).
- **Patients:** Medicare patients aged 65 and older, traditional Medicare and MA, in the top 30% by readmission risk at discharge. About 100 in the first 12 months (half strength), about 200 a year after that.
- **Components:** medication review before discharge, nurse contact within 72 hours, clinic visit within 7 days.
- **Budget:** $400 variable cost per enrolled patient; $25,000 fixed cost per hospital per year.
- **Monthly measures:** 30-day all-cause readmissions of enrolled vs comparison patients, by payer; 30-day ED treat-and-release visits and observation stays; 30-day mortality; share of enrolled patients who received all three components; cost per enrolled patient.

### 9.3 Comparison group, not before-and-after

A before-and-after comparison would credit the program with regression to the mean (Section 3.4). In the Camden hotspotting trial, a naive pre-post comparison suggested a 38-point fall in readmissions, while the randomized comparison showed none (Finkelstein 2020). Use a concurrent comparison group:
- **Within the pilot hospitals.** In the first 12 months the program runs at half strength, so only about half of eligible patients can be enrolled. Offer places by a fixed rule set in advance (for example, alternate discharge days). Eligible patients not offered a place then form the comparison group, at no extra cost.
- **Across hospitals.** Add similar higher-risk heart failure and pneumonia patients at the six non-pilot hospitals over the same months, risk-adjusted.

**What month 12 can and cannot show.** About 100 patients will be enrolled by month 12, and about 31% of them would be readmitted without the program. So the month-12 estimate of the effect will be imprecise: even with a comparison group four times larger, the 95% interval for the relative reduction would run from about −25% to +38%. Month 12 can test delivery, cost and safety, and it can catch a program that is not working. It cannot confirm a 14% effect; that would take about 1,700 enrolled patients.[^power] The decision to make the program permanent should weigh the pilot's estimate together with the published evidence. Payer co-funding lowers the bar the pilot has to clear.

### 9.4 Month-12 go/no-go

Table 16. Month-12 decision rule. The VP of Quality and the CFO decide on a readout from quality analytics; the cost thresholds are the base case, and the effect thresholds come from Table 13.

| Test | Continue into year 2 if | Stop if |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery | ≥ 80% of enrolled patients received all three components | below 80% after a 3-month fix period |
| Enrollment | ≥ 90 patients enrolled in months 1–12 | fewer than 90 |
| Cost | ≤ $400 variable cost per enrolled patient and ≤ $25,000 fixed cost per hospital | above either |
| Safety | no rise vs the comparison group in 30-day ED visits, observation stays or mortality | a rise in any |
| Effect (direction only) | enrolled patients' 30-day readmission rate below the comparison group's | at or above it |

**Making the program permanent.** Decide at month 12, or at month 24 (about 300 patients) if the month-12 estimate is positive but below the thresholds. Make it permanent only if the measured reduction is at least 14% at no more than $400 per patient, at least 10% with a signed agreement returning at least 27% of payer savings, or at least 10% at no more than $236 per patient. **Extending beyond the two sites** needs payer co-funding in any case: the other six hospitals return $0.36 per $1 or less at the base effect, and $0.62 or less at a 20% effect.
