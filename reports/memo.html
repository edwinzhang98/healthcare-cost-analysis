Memo: Medicare readmission penalties and the proposed follow-up program

To: VP of Quality, CFO · From: Quality Analytics · Date: November 2026

Bottom line

  • Do not fund a follow-up program for all eight hospitals. It would cost about $1.1 million a year and win back only about $0.3 million in Medicare penalties.
  • Medicare's readmission penalty cost us an estimated $1.2 million in the 2026 payment year; two hospitals carried 62% of it: Princeton (Plainsboro), mostly hip and knee replacement, and Inspira Mullica Hill, mostly heart failure. This year's cuts, already set, total about $0.4 million.
  • If we want fewer readmissions as a quality goal, start small: a two-year pilot at Inspira Mullica Hill and Palisades, where heart failure is worst. It costs about $90,000 in year one and $130,000 a year at full size, still losing about $33,000 a year; penalty relief starts in late 2028. It breaks even if payers, Medicare included, return about 27% of their savings, so sign those terms and check both sites in Medicare's reports first.

Why

  1. Each prevented readmission costs more than it returns. Even at the two pilot hospitals, preventing one costs about $18,000 in program spending against about $15,000 of penalty relief (group-wide: about $27,000 against $7,000), and the $17,000 saved on the avoided stay goes to the insurer, not to us.
  2. Our gaps are specific. Seven of our eight hospitals readmit more than similar hospitals; five are in the bottom 20%. Heart failure at Inspira Mullica Hill, Palisades and Jersey City is worse than at 90% of similar hospitals. Good follow-up programs prevent about 1 in 10 readmissions; phone calls alone show no measurable effect.
  3. Penalties move slowly and swing on their own. Medicare looks back two years, so a program starting in January 2027 first lowers our penalty in late 2028 and reaches its full effect only in late 2031. This year's cuts fell at five of our hospitals, including the two largest, held steady at one, and rose at Palisades and Jersey City.
