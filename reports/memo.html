Memo: Medicare readmission penalties and the proposed follow-up program

To: VP of Quality, CFO · From: Quality Analytics · Date: November 2026 Re: Which hospitals drive our readmission penalties, how we compare, and whether a post-discharge follow-up program pays for itself

Bottom line

  • We lose an estimated $1.2 million a year to Medicare's readmission penalty. Two hospitals, Princeton (Plainsboro) and Inspira Mullica Hill, account for 62% of it.
  • A follow-up program at all eight hospitals would cost about $1.1 million a year and win back only about $0.3 million in penalties. We do not recommend funding it on financial grounds.
  • If we want to act on readmissions now, start small: a 12-month pilot at Inspira Mullica Hill and Palisades for higher-risk heart failure and pneumonia patients. It would cost about $130,000 a year, with a clear rule for whether to expand.

What we found

  1. Where the money goes. Heart failure ($511,000) and hip/knee replacement ($327,000) drive most of our penalty. Medicare charges about $217,000 in penalties for each hip/knee readmission above the expected level, against about $42,000 for heart failure. A few surgical readmissions are therefore expensive.
  2. How we compare. Seven of our eight hospitals readmit more patients than similar hospitals elsewhere, and five are in the worst fifth. The gaps are specific: heart failure at Inspira Mullica Hill, Palisades and Jersey City; COPD at four hospitals; hip/knee at Princeton. New Jersey as a whole readmits more patients than almost any other state.
  3. Why a program won't pay for itself. Good follow-up programs prevent roughly 1 in 10 readmissions among the patients they reach. Programs built only on phone calls prevent none. Penalty relief also arrives late: Medicare looks back two years, so a program starting in January 2027 first lowers our penalty in late 2028 and is fully counted only from late 2030. Penalties also swing on their own: this year's penalties fell sharply at most of our hospitals and roughly doubled at Palisades.
  4. What would change the answer. The pilot breaks even if it prevents at least 14% of readmissions among the patients it enrolls, if it costs less than about $236 per patient, or if insurers return about 30% of what they save. Insurers save about $17,000 for every readmission we prevent.
